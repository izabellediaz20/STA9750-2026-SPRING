Overarching Question and My Contribution

This report is one of five individual analyses contributing to the team project Restaurant Complaints vs Inspection Risk in NYC. The project’s overarching question asks whether neighborhoods with higher complaint activity experience systematically worse restaurant inspection outcomes. My teammates have established that the answer is yes in both cross-sectional and persistence terms, and that the relationship is contemporaneous rather than predictive. My contribution refines this finding by asking a more precise question: are all types of food-related 311 complaints equally informative about food safety risk, or do certain complaint categories carry substantially stronger signals about underlying violations?

This question matters for both scientific and practical reasons. On the scientific side, understanding which complaint types most strongly predict critical violations tells us something about the mechanisms connecting citizen observations to regulatory outcomes. If rodent complaints are the strongest predictor, it suggests that visible pest activity (something residents can directly observe from outside an establishment) is the most reliable signal of internal food safety failure. If food establishment complaints are strongest, it suggests that broader, more diffuse citizen concerns about restaurant conditions are more informative than specific observable events. On the practical side, if certain complaint types carry stronger signals, regulators could develop a weighted complaint scoring system that prioritizes inspection responses to the most predictive complaint categories, improving the efficiency of enforcement resource allocation without requiring any new data collection.

The 311 dataset used in this project contains four food-related complaint categories: Rodent, Food Establishment, Food Poisoning, and Unsanitary Condition. These categories differ substantially in their volume, specificity, and relationship to the regulatory framework. Rodent complaints, the most numerous at 160,737 over the study period, reflect visible pest activity that residents can observe directly. Food Establishment complaints (50,604) are a broad catch-all category that encompasses general concerns about restaurant conditions. Food Poisoning complaints (12,586) reflect the most direct kind of harm: a resident who believes they became ill after eating at a restaurant. Unsanitary Condition complaints (1,263) are the rarest and most heterogeneous category, covering cleanliness concerns that may or may not be restaurant-specific.

My specific question is which types of food-related complaints are most strongly associated with higher neighborhood-level critical violation rates. My hypothesis entering the analysis is that Rodent complaints will show the strongest correlation with critical violations, for two reasons. First, rodent presence is itself a critical violation category under DOHMH’s inspection rubric, meaning that a confirmed rodent infestation that generates citizen complaints will likely also generate critical violation citations when an inspector visits. Second, rodent complaints are the most objectively verifiable type: a resident who reports seeing a rat or mouse near a restaurant is reporting something specific and observable rather than a general subjective concern about the establishment’s cleanliness.

fetch_all_socrata <- function(dataset_id, query = "",
                               select = NULL, page_size = 50000) {
  base_url <- glue("https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/{dataset_id}.json")
  offset <- 0; pages <- list()
  repeat {
    req <- request(base_url) |>
      req_url_query(`$limit` = page_size, `$offset` = offset, `$where` = query)
    if (!is.null(select)) req <- req |> req_url_query(`$select` = select)
    page <- req |> req_retry(max_tries = 3) |> req_perform() |>
      resp_body_json(simplifyVector = TRUE) |> as_tibble()
    if (nrow(page) == 0) break
    message(glue("  {dataset_id}: {offset + nrow(page)} rows"))
    pages[[length(pages) + 1]] <- page
    if (nrow(page) < page_size) break
    offset <- offset + page_size
  }
  bind_rows(pages)
}

Data Quality Assessment

The complaint type analysis faces a specific data quality challenge that does not affect the aggregate analyses: the unequal sample sizes across complaint categories create different levels of statistical reliability for each correlation estimate. With 160,737 Rodent complaints distributed across 183 ZIP codes, the Rodent correlation estimate is based on a large, stable sample. With only 1,263 Unsanitary Condition complaints across 146 ZIP codes, the Unsanitary Condition correlation estimate is much noisier and more sensitive to individual observations. This unequal reliability should be kept in mind when interpreting the relative magnitudes of the four correlation coefficients.

A second quality consideration is the specificity of each complaint category relative to restaurants specifically. All four complaint types were filtered from the 311 dataset based on their category label, but not all complaints within each category necessarily relate to a restaurant. Rodent complaints, for example, may refer to rodent activity in a residential building, a street, or a park rather than a restaurant. The 311 data does not contain a field that reliably identifies whether a complaint was filed about a food service establishment, though the Food Establishment and Food Poisoning categories are more likely to be restaurant-specific than Rodent and Unsanitary Condition. This differential specificity creates a potential source of attenuation bias: if Rodent complaints are less restaurant-specific than Food Establishment complaints, their correlation with restaurant inspection outcomes would be attenuated relative to the true underlying relationship. This may explain why rodent complaints, despite being directly tied to a major critical violation category, show a lower correlation with inspection outcomes than Food Establishment complaints.

Rodent complaints account for 71% of all food-related 311 activity, making it the dominant signal by volume. Food Establishment complaints represent 22%, Food Poisoning about 6%, and Unsanitary Condition less than 1%. This volume imbalance has two implications for the analysis. First, the ZIP-level correlation estimates will be most reliable for Rodent and Food Establishment complaints, which have sufficient volume to produce stable ZIP-level counts, and least reliable for Unsanitary Condition, which averages fewer than 10 complaints per ZIP over the entire study period. Second, any aggregate complaint-outcome relationship documented by the team’s cross-sectional analysts will be disproportionately driven by Rodent complaint volume since it constitutes such a large fraction of total complaints. The type-specific analysis here tests whether Rodent complaints are actually the type carrying the most signal, or whether the smaller-volume types punch above their weight.

The seasonal pattern chart reveals important differences in temporal behavior across complaint types. This seasonal heterogeneity is relevant because it means the correlation between Rodent complaint volume and inspection outcomes may partly capture seasonal patterns rather than pure food safety signal, while the correlations for more temporally stable complaint types may be cleaner measures of the underlying relationship.

Findings and Interpretation

The correlation results produce a finding that directly contradicts the initial hypothesis. Food Establishment complaints, not Rodent complaints, show the strongest correlation with critical violation rates at the ZIP level (r = 0.157). Rodent complaints come second at r = 0.116, followed by Food Poisoning at r = 0.109, and Unsanitary Condition at r = 0.047. All four complaint types show positive correlations with both the critical violation rate and the mean inspection score, and negative correlations with the grade-A rate, confirming that all four types point in the same direction even if their magnitudes differ. The directional consistency across all three outcome measures for all four complaint types is itself an important finding: it rules out the possibility that the positive correlations are artifacts of a particular outcome measure and suggests a genuine underlying relationship between complaint activity and food safety risk regardless of how that risk is measured.

The stronger performance of Food Establishment complaints relative to Rodent complaints warrants careful explanation. The Food Establishment category captures a wide and heterogeneous range of citizen concerns about restaurant conditions, from visible cleanliness and food handling practices to concerns about ingredient freshness, employee hygiene, and general sanitation. This breadth may actually be an advantage: because the category encompasses such a wide range of concerns, a high Food Establishment complaint rate in a ZIP code signals a broadly elevated level of concern about restaurant conditions across multiple dimensions, rather than a single specific problem. Rodent complaints, by contrast, are specific to one type of sanitation failure, and while rodent presence is directly cited as a critical violation, it is a narrower signal than the broad-based concern captured by Food Establishment complaints.

The relatively strong performance of Food Poisoning complaints (r = 0.109) is notable given the small volume of this complaint type. Food Poisoning complaints reflect the most direct possible evidence of food safety failure: a consumer who became ill after eating at a restaurant. The fact that this small-volume complaint type produces correlations nearly as large as the much higher-volume Rodent category suggests that Food Poisoning complaints may carry a very high per-complaint signal-to-noise ratio. Each Food Poisoning complaint represents a confirmed health outcome rather than a precautionary observation, making it a direct rather than indirect indicator of the kind of food safety failures that inspectors are trained to identify.

Unsanitary Condition complaints show the weakest association (r = 0.047), which is consistent with the heterogeneity of this category. Unsanitary Condition complaints include concerns about street cleanliness, garbage disposal, and conditions in non-food establishments, meaning that many complaints in this category may have no relationship to restaurant food safety outcomes. The attenuation caused by this category-level imprecision would naturally produce weaker correlations with restaurant inspection outcomes.

The scatter grid confirms that all four correlations reflect genuine distributed trends rather than being driven by outliers. The positive relationship between complaint count and critical violation rate holds broadly across the range of ZIP code complaint counts for each type, with no obvious structural breaks or outlier clusters driving the results.

Connection to the Overarching Question

This analysis adds specificity to the team’s answer to the overarching question. The overarching question asks whether complaint activity is associated with worse inspection outcomes, and the cross-sectional analyses confirm that it is. My contribution reveals that this association is not uniform across complaint types: Food Establishment complaints carry the strongest signal despite not being the most common complaint type, while Unsanitary Condition complaints carry relatively little signal despite covering food safety concerns. This finding refines the policy implications of the project. Rather than treating all food-related 311 complaints as equivalent signals, regulators could develop type-weighted prioritization systems that give greater weight to Food Establishment complaints, which appear to be the most reliable indicators of underlying critical violation rates. This type-weighted approach could improve inspection targeting efficiency without requiring any new data collection infrastructure, since the 311 system already disaggregates complaints by type.
